Google Business Profile for firms in the Gulf: the fields that actually matter

2 June 2026 · 7 min read

Why this beats the website in year one

For a firm with a physical office and a defined service area, the Google Business Profile usually produces more contact than the website does in the first six months. It appears above the search results, it carries a tap-to-call button, and it takes days rather than months to have an effect. It is also free, which makes the fact that most firms fill in three fields and abandon it genuinely strange.

Categories are the single most important field

Your primary category determines which searches you are eligible to appear for. Get it wrong and nothing else you do will help. A firm that does civil contracting and picks the generic construction company category will lose to a competitor who picked the specific one.

Choose one primary category that describes your main revenue line, then add secondary categories for genuine service lines only. Padding the list with categories you do not really serve dilutes relevance rather than expanding reach.

Service areas, set honestly

If you serve Muscat and Salalah, list those. Listing every governorate in the country to appear everywhere tends to weaken your standing in the places you actually operate. Google is trying to answer a local query, and a business that claims the whole country reads as less local than one that claims a city.

The fields most firms skip

  • Services, listed individually with short descriptions. Each one is a matchable term.
  • Business description, written for a person rather than stuffed with keywords.
  • Opening hours, including special hours for Ramadan and public holidays. Wrong hours produce a wasted trip and a bad review.
  • Photographs, geotagged, showing the office, the team and completed projects. Profiles with recent photographs get materially more engagement than those without.
  • Products, which for a contractor works well as project types with images.
  • Attributes, such as whether the premises are accessible or which languages your staff speak.

Reviews are the ranking factor you control

Volume, recency and response rate all matter. A profile with nine reviews from last year performs worse than one with the same nine spread across the past three months, because recency signals an operating business.

Build the request into your process rather than running occasional campaigns. The moment to ask is at handover, when the client is most satisfied, and the request should come from the person they worked with rather than from a generic account. Reply to every review, including the negative ones, in the same language it was written in.

One practical note for bilingual markets: your business name should be written exactly as it appears on your licence and signage. Adding keywords to the name is a violation, and competitors in the Gulf report each other for it regularly.

Arabic and English on one profile

Google will serve your profile to users searching in both languages. Write the description in the language most of your clients search in, and make sure your services list uses the terms people actually type, which in Gulf markets is frequently a mix of Arabic and transliterated English. Keyword research done for Malaysia will not transfer.

What to check monthly

  1. New reviews, answered within a few days
  2. Search terms report, to see which queries surfaced you
  3. Photograph count, with something new added each month
  4. Suggested edits from the public, which can silently change your hours or address

Fifteen minutes a month is enough. The profile is not a project, it is a maintained asset, and the firms that treat it that way are the ones sitting above you in the map results.
